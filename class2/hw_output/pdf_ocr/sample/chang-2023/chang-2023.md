## A B S T R A C T

Rockfall tracking and mass estimation are critical for effective hazard response. While seismic techniques have been applied to the field, systematic analysis remains lacking. In this study, we conducted rockfall experiments using seismic networks to investigate tracking and mass estimation issues. Two methods, time-based and amplitude-based source location methods, were used to identify initial rock-ground impact points. The highfitness areas (>0.95) overlapping between the two methods showed a location error of <8.7 m for initial rock-ground impact points. However, the combined methods could not track bounce points on the slope. To address the limitation, we further defined a seismic energy ratio, a ratio between target and reference stations. When a reference station was far away from rockfall, the rising or decreasing of high-frequency (70–99 Hz) seismic energy ratios reflected the rock approaching or departing from the target station, respectively. Additionally, the peak low-frequency (1–40 Hz) seismic energy ratio of ground stations indicated the timing of the initial rock-ground impact. Successful implementation of these methods depends on optimal station distribution. The study recommends that different source-to-station distances with triangular station geometry and stations at different elevations provide a reliable location determination and straightforward rockfall tracking. Furthermore, the lowest frequency(f ) satisfied the power spectral threshold of − 100 dB during the rockfall moving process correlated with the rock mass(M), M = 38,989FL-2.03 with R2 of 0.62. The relation was practical for estimating rockfalls' mass for future monitoring in the same slope. Our experiments demonstrate that this seismic technique can effectively assist in rockfall assessment.

## 1. Introduction

Rockfall is a rock mass detaching from a cliff by progressive defor mation before failure. When it starts moving, the motion behavior is composed of falling, sliding, rolling, and bouncing, which are deter mined by the slope angle, except for sliding (Ritchie, 1963). If the slope angle is <45◦, rock tends to roll on the slope. During the rolling process, a rock contains moving and rotating behaviors whose route is almost linear and whose edges contact the slope continuously (Erismann, 1986). If the slope angle, however, increases above 45◦, a rock falls and then interacts with the slope, exhibiting a bouncing behavior. The moving path of the rock inherits high uncertainty due to many influ ential factors, such as the material of rocks and slope, incident angle, and shape of rocks (Giani et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2018). Rock bouncing may be fragmented, especially for metamorphic rocks, breaking along their foliation. (Giacomini et al., 2009). Next, when a slope angle is above 60◦ to 70◦, a rock changes its motion behavior into a fall. These rock motion behaviors from start to end are defined as trajectories. Small rock travels a shorter trajectory than large rock because of the smaller kinetic energy, barriers on the motion path and gentle slope (Dorren, 2003; Asteriou and Tsiambaos, 2016; Lu et al., 2021). In general, the trajectory of all rocks is controlled by the topography (Chen et al.,

2013a).

Rock contact with terrain causes ground vibration, which can be recorded by nearby seismometers as rockfall signals. In recent decades, much research has relied on the rockfall signal to understand its motion behavior by monitoring a target slope or conducting experiments on a slope scale (Vilajosana et al., 2008; Helmstetter and Garambois, 2010; Hibert et al., 2017; Guinau et al., 2019; Le Roy et al., 2019; Iannucci et al., 2020). The seismic feature of rockfall sometimes presents pulse features of its waveform and time-frequency spectrogram (Walter et al., 2012; Hibert et al., 2017). Based on the time difference between two pulses, the falling height of a rock can be roughly estimated (Dietze et al., 2017). Furthermore, the fragmentation process of rock produces a higher frequency signal than nonfragmentation (Salo ´ et al., 2018). Then, large rock interacting with the ground generates a lower frequency signal than small rock (Huang et al., 2007). The source-station distance influences those observation frequency ranges. Typically, it is up to 200 Hz with a distance of <100 m (Zimmer and Sitar, 2015; Hibert et al., 2017).

Except for the aforementioned seismic typology, few studies have attempted to account for rockfall trajectory and mass estimation using seismic analysis, which are crucial issues for emergency response and rockfall assessment. Feng et al. (2021) utilized the time difference of the rockfall signal from each station to locate every roll/bounce point. However, a complex geological background with path and site effects influences transmission velocity and recorded amplitudes of seismic signals, presenting inconsistent performances in determining the source location. Furthermore, the station distribution from the research was only located on the upslope area, and rocks moved downslope. The poor station coverage of the rockfall trajectory may affect the location per formance. Thus, locating bounce and falling points on the ground is still an unsolved problem in rockfall monitoring. In addition to the timebased method (TB) for source location, the amplitude-based method (ASL), which has not yet been applied in a location of slope scale rockfall, is another practical way to determine the source location (Walsh et al., 2017). Here, we then discuss their pros and cons, offering a method for reliable location results. This method suggests a strategy of station distribution for rockfall monitoring in advance. Moreover, for the rock mass issue, Hibert et al. (2017) adopted a single boulder experiment and indicated that the seismic signal amplitude correlates to the rock's momentum. Nevertheless, obtaining information about the velocity of moving rock in actual rockfall events is lacking. Additional parameters need to be taken into account for practical application. Huang et al. (2007) found that the size of the boulder relates to the extension of the seismic signal towards the low-frequency band, a feature that could be a potential tool for rock mass estimation. We have included this concept in this study.

Overall, the goals of this paper are (i) to locate rockfall bounce sites and initial rock-ground impact points on the slope/ground, (ii) to compare the pros and cons of the two methods for location determina tion, (iii) to propose a new way to track rockfall movement and (iv) to construct a regression between seismic parameters and rock masses. We utilized field rockfall experiments of single boulders and sledgehammer impact as a means of obtaining these goals.

## 2. Background information

## 2.1. Experimental location and equipment distribution

The experiment took place on a slope near the 9.8 K milestone of the Provincial Highway 9D, Taiwan (Fig. 1a), on three different dates, November 8, 2019, November 4, 2020, and December 3, 2020, with different geophone deployments. On November 8, 2019, only four geophones, G01 to G04, were set in the field. G01 was close to the experimental area, and G02 was on the ground. G03 and G04 were far from the experimental area. One was above the launch location, and the other was on the ground. The sampling rate of the four stations was 200 Hz. On November 4, 2020, and December 3, 2020, we added G05 on the slope and a linear geophone array, A01 to A07, with a five-meter spacing distance for each station on the ground. The stations' sampling rates were set to 400 Hz. All seismic stations were three-component geo phones (PE-6/B, 0.4 Hz to Nyquist frequency 200 Hz) with data loggers (DATA- CUBE3 Type 2). The logger was configured with an amplifier grain of sixteen. An external GPS antenna was used to precisely syn chronize the time of the measured records at the different stations with a clock accuracy of 10− 6 s. Vertical component data were considered for location issues, sledgehammer experiments, earthquake signals, and mass estimation issues, and three component data were for seismic en ergy ratio(R). We also installed two cameras, one the maintenance trail and one the ground near A01, to photograph rocks moving on the upper slope (Fig. 1b) and downslope (Fig. 1c), respectively. Additionally, an airborne drone recorded the complete experimental process (Fig. 1d). DJI Phantom4 RTK and Autel EVO II PRO RTK drones captured highprecision positioning of vertical and inclined aerial photos on May 4, 2020, and December 15, 2021. They provided input to photogrammetry software Pix4D-generated orthoimages and a digital surface model (DSM) with the EPSG 3826 coordinate system. Their resolution was on average 2.15 cm/pixel for constructing slope maps, contours, and pro files. We then resampled the grid resolution to 20 cm/pixel due to the efficiency of retrieving the source location.

We randomly selected 32 rocks, ranging from 4 kg to 55 kg, in the field and marked each by date and rock number (Table S1). For example, rock 1203R4 is rock no. 4 on December 3, 2020. Then, we carried rocks to the narrow platform at an elevation of 391 m and freely dropped them. Rocks were launched along the gentle slope with a heading of 168◦ (north: 0◦) rather than on the steep terrain on the east side to prevent rocks from jumping to the road (Fig. S1). Then, they collided with the terrain barrier and tended to change the direction of movement to 103◦. We constructed two profiles according to the rockfall's starting point to the endpoint: A-A' from the release location to G05 (Fig. 1e) and B-B′ from G01 to A01 (Fig. 1f). Three zones could be determined by the angle of slope and the aspect of the experimental area. Zone I, with exposed quartz-mica schist was on the upper slope of the A-A' profile, with a slope angle of approximately 43◦. The moving behavior of rocks tended towards rolling. Zone II was composed of two different aspect areas: a lower part of A-A' with a slope angle of approximately 53◦ and the upper part of B-B′ with a slope angle of approximately 42◦. As a rock rolled from Zone I to enter Zone II, it would fall and then contact the slope, turning into a bounce/roll behavior. Zone III was on the lower part of B-B′ with a slope angle of approximately 49◦ encouraging the bouncing behavior of rocks. Zone III contained three steps covered with some small rocks. In addition, the ground close to the foot of the slope accumulated some rock boulders/debris.

## 2.2. Investigation of effects of source, path, and site conditions

Seismometers can record the ground vibrations caused by a rock's interaction with a slope. Recorded seismograms result from the convolution of time-series functions corresponding to the effect of the source, path and site. The source effect is characteristic of different seismic sources. The path effect is dominated by the medium along the propagation path that produces seismic attenuation. The site effect may amplify (or deamplify) the seismic amplitude when the seismic wave propagates through the near-surface material. To acquire background knowledge of the three effects in this experimental area, we utilized two rockfall experiments to examine the source effect, sledgehammer impacts to understand the path effect, and one local earthquake to extract the site correction of each station.

To understand the effect of the source, considering the same impact location of the rock mass, 30 kg 1203R4 and 25 kg 1203R5 in the early stage of the experiments were selected to analyze their impact signals. The waveform of the 1203R4 impact without fragmentation showed a diamond shape (Fig. S2a). The impact waveform with fragmentation from 1203R5 presented a kite shape (Fig. S2b). The signals rapidly peaked and gradually decayed, inheriting different features from the signal without fragmentation. Their dominant frequencies were 76 Hz and 80 Hz, respectively. The fragmentation signals of rock 1203R5 contributed to the second dominant frequencies of 148 Hz. The frequency feature was documented by G02 as well. The fragmentation behavior recorded at G02 contained a higher frequency domain of 49.0 Hz and 84.8 Hz than non-fragmentation with a frequency of 44.6 Hz and 67.1 Hz (Fig. S2), which was also observed by Salo ´ et al. (2018).

For the path effect, the experiments were held on November 4, 2020, and December 3, 2020, with five-day antecedent precipitation of 22.5 mm and 836.5 mm, respectively. The rain gauge of C1U840 maintained by the Central Weather Bureau (CWB), Taiwan is 1.94 km from the research site. For experiments, we used a 7-kg sledgehammer striking an iron plate (Fig. S3) to generate artificial vibration sources immediately adjacent to A01 and G01. The artificial vibration signal was transmitted through the ground sediment slower than through part sediment and bedrock slope. The apparent velocity of the former ranged from 558 m/s to 673 m/s, and the latter ranged from 1052 m/s to 1123 ${ \mathfrak { m } } / { \mathfrak { s } } ,$ almost twice the apparent velocity of the former (Fig. 2). The difference in apparent velocity resulted from the air void ratio. The apparent velocity would be higher when the seismic wave propagated in media with a lower air void ratio. Similar to the apparent velocity between G01 and G03, the media of the propagation path was composed of pure quartzmica schist; it inherited the highest apparent velocity of approxi mately 1391 m/s. Furthermore, the apparent velocity fluctuated with precipitation, from 10.7% to 12% on the date with high antecedent precipitation. However, not all paths between two stations inherited the same feature. The apparent velocity between G01 and G04 was faster on the date with low antecedent precipitation. The sledgehammer experi ment supported the complexity of apparent velocity, which was sensi tive to a different composition of path media and slightly influenced by rainfall infiltration.

In the site effect, we selected an earthquake from November 5, 2020, reported by the CWB, whose epicentral distance was 4.06 km and focal depth was 11.70 km from the east of the study area. Compared to the distance of seismic wave propagation, the distance between station apertures could be ignored; hence, we assumed that all the propagation paths from the earthquake to the seismic stations at the incident angle of 12.81◦ (0◦ for vertical incident) were the same. Only the near-surface material influenced those seismic signals. We examined the first peak ground velocity (PGV) after high pass filtering with corner 4 for the rockfalls' hosted frequencies of 10 Hz–99 Hz of the P wave to the earthquake (Table 1). Then, taking the G02 as a reference station, each station's PGV was divided by G02's PGV as a correction factor for the site condition (sc) of each station.

## 3.2. Time-based method

The time-based method(TB) was previously approached with loca tion distances of hundreds of meters (Reinwald et al., 2021) and kilo meters scale monitoring (Burtin et al., 2013). We modified the concept for less than a one-hundred-meter monitoring scale. The seismic signal generated by a source was first received by the nearest station at $\mathbf { t } _ { \mathrm { i } 0 }$ with a source-to-station distance ${ \bf r } _ { \mathrm { i 0 } }$ and recorded by other stations at t with a source-to-station distance $\mathbf { r } _ { \mathrm { i } } ,$ respectively. A cross-correlation function determined the time difference between each station and the nearest station of $\mathbf { t } _ { \mathrm { i - i 0 } }$ with the assumption that the wave propagation speed was constant. Therefore, for different pairs of stations between the nearest station and the i station, their ratio of $\Delta \mathrm { r } _ { \mathrm { i - i 0 } } \mathrm { t o } \Delta \mathrm { t } _ { \mathrm { i - i 0 } }$ should be the same. We further adapted a least square scheme for total analyzed grids (j) (Eq. (5)) and excluded the results of grids with an unreasonable parameter, negative $\Delta \mathbf { r } _ { \mathrm { i j - i j } 0 } .$ . The remaining results were for the misfit function inheriting the same algorithm as ASL by discrepancies between calcu lated and theoretical distance differences between the nearest station and the i-th station. Then, the relative fitness and the best result could be defined.

$$
\mathrm { v ( r ) } = { \frac { \mathrm { r _ { i j } } - \mathrm { r _ { i j 0 } } } { \mathrm { t _ { i } } - \mathrm { t _ { i 0 } } } } = { \frac { \Delta \mathrm { r _ { i j - i j 0 } } } { \Delta \mathrm { t _ { i - i 0 } } } }\tag{5}
$$

The distance from the source to the station is a crucial parameter for ASL and TB. In an actual event, the seismic waves can propagate through underground material to stations. To simplify the propagation distance, we used (1) the Euclidean distance between each grid on DSM and each station and (2) the topographic distance as a source-to-station distance. To obtain the topographic distance, a vertical plane (dip: 90◦) was created between two analytical points. Next, all DSM grids were pro jected onto the vertical plane, and a threshold of point-to-plane distance was set. Then, grids that meet the thresholds of 0.1 m, meaning grid points with a vertical plane distance <0.1 m, constituted the terrain profile between two analytical points. Finally, the Euclidean distance between those grids was summed as the topographic distance. We used the topographic distance as the source-to-station distance in ASL and TB for bounce locations on the slope. However, for initial rock-ground impact points and sledgehammer experiment on the ground, we only took a Euclidean distance between each grid and station as the sourceto-station distance rather than the topographic distance because the ground vegetation enlarged the distance from the source to the station by taking the topographic distance.

## 3.3. Ratio of seismic energy

Except for the source location, we further presented a new method in the following process to downplay the source effect, which eliminated signal features caused by the source effect (e.g., variances in rock mass). First, we integrate the squared amplitudes for a 0.1-s time window length (T), overlapping by 50%, to be seismic energy (E), as shown in $\operatorname { E q . }$ (6). Furthermore, the seismic energy ratio (R) can be calculated by the seismic energy of the station i divided by a reference station (ref) that is far from the rockfall $\left( \operatorname { E q } . \left( 7 \right) \right)$ . In this experiment, the rockfall gradually moved away from G03; thus, G03 was chosen as the reference.

$$
\mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { k } } ( \mathrm { t } _ { c } ) = \sum _ { \tau = t _ { c } - \frac { T } { 2 } } ^ { t _ { c } + \frac { T } { 2 } } [ ( h ( \tau ) ) ] ^ { 2 } \Delta \mathrm { t }\tag{6}
$$

$$
\mathsf { R } _ { \mathrm { k } } ^ { \mathrm { i } } = \frac { \mathsf { E } _ { \mathrm { k } } ^ { \mathrm { i } } } { \mathsf { E } _ { \mathrm { k } } ^ { \mathrm { r e f } } }\tag{7}
$$

where k is the component of seismic data, Δt is sampling interval, h (t) is the time series of seismic signals, $\mathbf { t } _ { \mathrm { c } }$ is the time center of the time window.

Previous studies have found that high-frequency signals refer to the dynamics of rockfall motion (Dietze et al., 2017; Hibert et al., 2017), and low-frequency signals are linked to a rebound of the contact surface between rock and terrain. (Huang et al., 2007). Therefore, we selected the high-frequency band 70 Hz–99 Hz (H) and the low-frequency band 1 Hz–40 Hz (L), which prevented overlap with the frequency band of ambient seismic noise (40 Hz–70 Hz shown in Fig. S4). The aforementioned two frequency bands for high and low seismic energy ratio (HR and LR) were applied in this research to track rockfall.

## 4. Results and discussion

## 4.1. Bounce location on the slope

One bounce of 1104R1(5.38 m far from G01) was used as an example (black star in Fig. 3) for locating a bounce point by ASL and TB through topographic distance. The bounce was the moment before 1104R1 changed the moving heading from 168◦ to 103◦. In the TB scheme, the seismic signal from the bounce first arrived at G01 and followed G05, G02, G03, and other ground stations. The TB result showed that when all stations were used for TB, the relative fitness range above 0.95, a threshold of reliable location result proposed by Chang et al. (2021), was a divergence pattern (orange color in Fig. 3). Although the TB can offer the best result by grid search, the diverse distribution of relative fitness indicated an unreliable location determination. There were two reasons for the dispersion pattern: (1) different apparent velocities through the bedrock or part of the bedrock and sediment, and (2) ground vegetation/ barriers enlarge the topographic distance from each searching grid to ground Stations A01 to A07 and G04, which is different than the topographic distance for slope stations. Consequently, we only considered using the stations on the slope (G01, G02, G03, and G05) to avoid these two factors.

The reliable area, however, was more dispersed (brown in Fig. 3). To understand the phenomenon, the arrival times of four stations were chosen (red line; Fig. S5). Then, we assumed that the apparent velocity from the bounce location to each station was the same as the velocity by sledgehammer impact from G01 to each station due to the small spatial shift of 5.38 m. The theoretical arrival time could be determined (blue line; Fig. S5). The discrepancy between these two arrival times showed that the bounce signal propagated faster than the impact signal. The velocity changed from 37% to 81%, corresponding to a longer topo graphic distance. This implied that a small spatial shifting of the source can produce a disparate velocity pattern, which causes difficulty in locating the signal source on the slope by the TB method.

Most of the amplitudes were within the range of $1 . 6 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \ : ( \mathrm { m } / s )$ to $1 . 7 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \ : ( \mathrm { m } / s )$ , except for the lower amplitudes at Stations A03 and A05. The display supported the small shifting of source location on the slope, exhibiting unpredictability. Additionally, the sledgehammer impacted the horizontal iron plate, but the rock contacted the slope with a different incident angle. The incident angle may play an uncertain role in the recorded signals—those bringing obstacles to locating the bounce point.

Next, we took the same test as the TB method. An arrangement of ASL excluded data from ground stations whose output was unacceptable as well (white color in Fig. 3). The result inherited the same pattern as the TB output, with a greater dispersion area of reliable areas. Therefore, locating the bounce point was still a challenge for repeated experiments. A detailed velocity model for a target slope may solve these issues (Kuehnert et al., 2020). However, the velocity model relies on seismic prospecting or geophysical surveys, which are difficult to conduct on a steep slope. Thus, it is necessary to access an efficient method for rockfall movement on the slope. We describe the issue in Section 4.3 using LR and HR.

## 4.2. Ground location of falling rocks

In addition to investigating the feasibility of locating the bounce points, the other objective was to determine the initial rock-ground impact, which might release significant kinetic energy causing the major hazard to the transportation system (Wei et al., 2014; Yan et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2021). In Section 4.1, we discussed how the dispersion pattern of high relative fitness areas could not offer robust outcomes; hence, the priority in this section was to confirm the reliable area convergence or not. Data on November 4, 2020, from all ground stations were selected. We manually recognized the initial rock-ground impact points using video screen captures and retrieved the signals from the substantial impact as input for TB and ASL. Both results presented convergence areas for initial rock-ground impact locations of falling rocks (Fig. S6), implying that both methods were suitable for this pur pose. The outcome would support the assumption that ground sediment was more homogeneous than slope bedrock, in which seismic waves obeyed the theoretical rule. Consequently, we included all experiments on November 4, 2020, with seven of eleven rocks falling on the ground (Table ST1) in locations close to the slope (Fig. 4).

In the TB scheme, the location error ranged from 2.14 m to 10.02 m (Fig. 4) with an average of 3.66 m, except for 1104R8, which had the highest location error. Almost all rocks landed northwest of Station A01; however, 1104R8 fell southwest of Station A01. The error of 1104R8 was derived from the poor station coverage of the source location, which is crucial in the velocity-based method (Chang et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the inverted velocity from the best location and source of TB was an average of 781.2 m/s and 705.7 m/s (Table S4), respectively. These apparent velocities were slightly faster than the sledgehammer impact range of 558.1 m/s to 670.3 m/s.

With the ASL scheme, the location error was on average 8.58 m (Table S4), and α was 0.045 of the best results. Then, the α retrieved from the source location was 0.039. These values are almost half of the value of the sledgehammer impact, indicating that the signals from falling rocks and the sledgehammer impact had different decay features. Next, α from the source location presented real cases of signal decay from the rock falling on the ground at this experimental site. We also recalculated ASL by fixing α as 0.039 to perform the inversion again. The result showed that the location error with fixing α substantially decreased to 6.47 m (Fig. 4). After fixing α for ASL, 1104R3 and 1104R6 still showed significant location error due to a similar source-to-station distance from sources to A01, A03, and G02. The precise result of ASL depended on the onset of the nearest station and different spacing of the source-to-station distances (Walsh et al., 2017). In two cases, the sourceto-station distance to G02, A01, and A03 was too close. They were 8.40 m, 6.68 m, and 8.42 m to 1104R3 and 8.50 m, 6.45 m, and 7.08 m to 1104R6. The distribution pattern caused a significant location error for the two rocks. Except for these two cases, ASL performed as well as the TB method. ASL can make up for a deficiency of TB, such as the lack of station coverage case 1104R8, reducing the location error to 5.85 m due to the good distribution of the source-to-station distance. This feature is applicable to the strategy for future monitoring.

Nevertheless, to use the location method for actual cases, the ASL and TB methods cannot be applied to all causes. To explore the practical operation, we reviewed the distribution of relative fitness over 0.90 of the results from the two methods (Fig. S6) and found that their reliable areas overlapped, suggesting both areas were partly the same. These grids may offer the possibility of combining ASL and TB with practical applications.

We first identified all grids from both methods with relative fitness over 0.95 as a threshold and extracted overlapping grids. However, not all cases, such as 1104R1, contained the overlapping area with relative fitness >0.95. We gradually decreased the threshold from 0.95 by in tervals of 0.01 until extracting the overlapping result of the two methods. Then, we calculated the mean and standard deviation of the location error to the source location for all overlapping grids. The location error ranged from 1.06 m to 8.69 m with an average of 3.90 m, which resulted in four cases being dropped except for 1104R2, 1104R3, and 1104R8 (Table S3). Nevertheless, these results were around the minimum location error of the two methods. Therefore, we concluded that the overlapping area obtained by stratifying thresholds of relative fitness could be the result of the ground location point of falling rocks, which was adequate for further application with a straightforward automatic function.

## 4.3. Rockfall tracking

Based on the ASL and TB results, there were two remaining issues: (1) the ability to track moving rocks on the slope and (2) finding a way to retrieve the exact time/signal of rock falling on the ground? The example of the 30-kg rock without fragmentation, 1108R1–2, illustrates these two issues with the R method. 1108R1–2 was released at 03:00:26.7 (UTC) and stopped at 03:00:35.0 (UTC) (Fig. 5). When the rock started moving in the rolling phase, the edge of the rock contacted the slope quickly with a short time interval, as shown in Zone I. Because the time intervals between each contact of the rolling phase were too short, approximately 0.4 s (average time interval from 1 to 7 in Fig. 5) determined from recognition peaks of HRG01, we could not obtain the precise location of the impact point from the video. Only the final impact point in Zone I could be located (Fig. S7a).

Then, 1108R1–2 fell in Zone II (Fig. S7b), becoming a bounce phase (Fig. S7b-d) until it fell onto the ground debris (Fig. S7e). The video noted five impact points, which also showed prominent peaks (P1-P5) in the HR range of 70 Hz–99 Hz. Before releasing 1108R1, the HR ranged from $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \dot { \mathrm { t } } \mathrm { o } 1 0 ^ { - 1 }$ of background noise (Fig. 5). When the rock started moving, HRG01 sharply rose to 102 in Zone I, peaked in Zone II, and then dropped to the background level after the rock fell onto the ground debris. Additionally, the time difference was greater than one second during P1 to P2 and P4 to P5, indicating the fall characteristic from Zone I to Zone II and Zone II to Zone III. $\mathrm { H R } ^ { \mathrm { G 0 2 } }$ also exhibited a similar pattern to $\mathrm { H R } ^ { \mathrm { G 0 1 } }$ . The main discrepancy between $\mathrm { H R } ^ { \mathrm { G 0 1 } }$ and $\mathrm { H R } ^ { \mathrm { G 0 2 } }$ was the timing of their peak HR value. The spikes of HRG01 gradually increased (green line; Fig. 5) and reached a peak at the P4 impact, then, the spikes of $\mathrm { H R } ^ { \mathrm { G 0 1 } }$ decreased (green dashed line; Fig. 5). For $\mathrm { H R } ^ { \mathrm { G 0 2 } }$ , the spikes gently rose to a peak (grey line; Fig. 5), showing that the rock was approaching G02. This phenomenon was related to the decay feature of high-frequency signals. Therefore, the up and down of the HR showed the rock gradually approaching or departing from the station. Compared to HRG01 and HRG02, HRG04 was stabilized due to the long source-tostation distance corresponding to the decay of the high-frequency signals.

However, the LR exhibited a distinct pattern (Fig. 6). The back ground noise of the LR ranged from $1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { t o } 1 0 ^ { - 1 }$ . The peak of P1 to P5 was approximately ten to one hundred times the background value. The level of increase was half of the HR. However, the signal of P5 to ground stations G02 and G04 pointed out the peak LR showing the greatest impact on the ground debris, which was ten thousand and a thousand times the value to the background level, respectively. Even though the LR did not indicate the impacts of rolling and bouncing, it presented the timing of rock falling on the ground.

These results demonstrate that the HR responds to rockfall dynamics such as falling, rolling, and bouncing. The ascending and declining trend of the HR offers information about the rock approaching or departing from a station. The peak LR on ground stations can mark the greatest impact of rocks falling on the ground. These impacted-induced seismic signals can be used as input for ASL and TB to provide initial rockground impact points of falling rocks.

## 4.4. Station deployment strategy

The above issues regarding the source location by ASL and TB and tracking rocks by HR and LR were influenced by the geometry of the seismic station distribution. For example, 1104R3 and 1104R6 fell near the center of G02, A01, and A04, causing a higher location error than other rocks by using ASL. 1104R8 was outside the station coverage with a location error of 10.02 m to TB. Furthermore, the ascending and declining features of the HR and the peak of the LR were based on the upslope reference station (G03). Thus, deploying a seismic network for a specific target is the key in practical applications.

We use the sledgehammer experiment at A04 as an example to describe the strategy of station distribution of ASL and TB. In ASL, it is crucial to offer good spacing distances from source to station. For example, when A01, A03, A05 and A07 were utilized for ASL, the source-to-station distance of A01 was equal to that of A07, the same as that observed for A03 and A05. The effectiveness was similar to applying data from only two stations (A01 or A07 with A03 or A05), producing a diverse reliable area (Fig. S8a). However, when we added one more station near the source, the reliable area converged (Fig. S8b), sup porting that the high-fitness grids could be dragged near the station with the highest amplitude. If we put a station close to the source, the ASL would perform well. In the actual environment, the station cannot always be next to the source. Therefore, giving different source-to-station distances will also be a solution for ASL, such as using G02 (12 m), A01 (15 m), A06 (10 m), and A07 (15 m) (Fig. S8c). Another situation was that all stations were on the same side of the source. The reliable areas were diverse (Fig. S8d), which can be improved by fixing α derived from repeated experiments (Fig. S8e). After that, the high relative fitness grids were near a station with the shortest source-to-station distance, strengthening the description in Fig. S8b that dragged high-fitness grids to the station with the highest amplitude. In $^ { \mathrm { T B , } }$ the key is the coverage of stations near the source. The configuration of linear stations from A01, A03, A05, and A07 for TB revealed an inaccurate result with two areas of high fitness grids (Fig. S9a), which cannot be revised by adding the nearest Station A04 (Fig. S9b). The only method for TB was good station coverage, such as using A01, A06, A07, and G02 to retrieve three different pairs, A01-A06, A07-A06, and G02-A06, to produce a better result (Fig. S9c).

The reference station influenced the trend of the R-value. In Section $^ { 5 , }$ the reference station was G03. The result could be modified once we changed the reference station to G04. Fig. S10 shows the HR with G04 as a reference station. $\mathrm { H R } ^ { \mathrm { G 0 1 } }$ peaked when the rock was in Zone I and then gradually decreased due to rock departure from G01 and approach to G04. The monitoring target can be a point source, such as a single rockfall or overhanging rockfall. Then, the strategy can be extended to the regular monitoring of rock slope failures or landslides that have already occurred in Taiwan (Chang et al., 2021). In addition, the R method is adjustable depending on local conditions (Fig. 7b), which relies on a detailed field survey and the modelling technique to understand the location of an unstable rock mass and its potential trajectory.

## 4.6. Future perspective

To enhance the generalizability of our research findings, several prerequisites and procedures must be taken into account to apply seismic techniques for source location, rockfall tracking, and mass estimation to other slopes (Fig. 10). Firstly, in terms of location, we suggest conducting drone surveys to generate digital elevation models, using a sledgehammer to clarify the path effects, and analyzing earth quake signals to correct site-specific effects. In adapting ASL and TB, site and path effects should also be considered, respectively. Ultimately, a reliable location for initial rock-ground impact is achieved by identi fying the overlapping area of both results with high relative fitness (>0.95). Secondly, avoiding frequency bands saturated with ambient noise for rockfall tracking by seismic energy ratio is essential. Con ducting HR and LR can help to identify the feature of the rock mass approaching/departing from a station and the timing of rock-ground impacts. Finally, the lowest frequency during the rockfall process and rock mass can be used in repeated experiments to build an empirical regression for future applications in the experimental site.

## 5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates a systematical experiment using seismic signal analysis for tracking rockfall and estimating its mass. The com bined location approach using time-based (TB) and amplitude-based (ASL) methods yielded location estimates for initial rock-ground impact points with an error of <8.7 m. To identify the target signals of rock-ground impact, we relied on the appearance of peaks in the lowfrequency seismic energy ratio (LR) of ground stations, with a reference station located on the upslope. However, locating bouncing points using a combined method on the slope remains challenging. An alternative method assisted us in understanding a rockfall's motion, including approaching or leaving seismic station locations. By changing the reference station, the operation was more flexible. These results were based on particular distributions of seismic stations. Different source-tostation distances with a triangular station geometry are suitable for locating initial rock-ground impact points by ASL and TB. Then, stations located at different elevations, on the up and down slopes, benefit from the ascending and declining pattern of a high-frequency seismic energy ratio (HR) and a peak of the LR, respectively. Regarding mass estima tion, contact of a large boulder with the ground/slope extends the sig nal's frequency to low-frequency bands. We set power spectral thresholds to extract f during the rockfall process and found that the lowest frequency correlates with the rock mass. However, these results depend on prior knowledge influenced by background geology, which varies across different target slopes. Therefore, we recommend con ducting rockfall experiments to retrieve these variations before adapting rockfall monitoring by seismic technique.
